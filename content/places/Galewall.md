# [[Galewall-Secrets|The Galewall]]

## Overview

The Galewall appears first as a dark band sitting across the western horizon, then as layered grey and slate-blue weather piled on itself with storm systems working inside it continuously. The chop picks up well before the boundary. The wind builds in stages. The water goes cold. Near the storm edge, the [[Ashwall-Islands|Ashwall Islands]] rise as cold black volcanic spires with cloud on their heights and surf white at their bases — the last stop for ships heading west, the first stop for ships coming back east. Sulphur vents mark the upper slopes. Most Galewall crews know the chain better than they know their home ports.

Crossing takes about three weeks with an experienced pilot and a hull that has done it before. The accepted loss rate is one in three. A pilot who loses less than that is considered good and is paid accordingly. Ships do it often enough to keep trade alive between the Scatter and the colonial homelands, and fail often enough that every port shrine on the western [[places/crown-islands/index|Crown Islands]] has a board with names on it. The Galewall is not a wall because it cannot be crossed. It is a wall because crossing changes the price of every mistake.

Storms kill more ships than monsters here, but wreckage feeds a food chain. [[beastiary/beasts/giant-shark|Giant sharks]] follow damaged hulls near the Ashwall lee. [[beastiary/sea-life/killer-whale|Killer whale]] pods work the cold water on both sides of the storm belt and will follow lifeboats and debris for hours — not always attacking, sometimes circling, which most survivors describe as worse. Crews tell high-weather stories about [[beastiary/monstrosity/roc|rocs]] riding the stormfronts above the volcanic spires. Some crews distinguish a rarer thing: [[arclight-phoenix|arclight phoenix]] light moving inside the storm, bright enough to draw lines across closed eyelids. Most pilots care less about proving the stories than about staying below deck when the shadow or flash passes over the sail.

Ashwall crews hold a belief the colonial weather-offices have never formally engaged with: the Galewall is not where arclight phoenixes live. It is what they make. Born inside the [[Ashwall-Islands|Ashwall]] volcanic vents and flying west into the weather, the birds charge the storm system continuously, sustaining it the way combustion sustains itself. The permanent storm belt is not a natural feature of the western ocean — it is the accumulated output of something that hatches in stone and dies in lightning and does both again. ^\[inferred]

The colonial academies have been trying to explain why the Galewall does not behave like weather for over a century. It does not move seasonally. It does not dissipate. Its energy profile is consistent in ways that frustrate meteorological models. Ashwall pilots do not find this confusing. They know what comes out of the vents, and they know what the Galewall looks like from inside, and the two descriptions match well enough that the question feels answered. ^\[inferred]

Ashwall crews carry a separate legend that colonial weather-offices don't engage with: the Stormwarden. A figure — never the same face twice across generations, always in a vessel too small for the conditions — that appears during the worst crossings and guides failing ships through. The instructions are practical rather than mystical: trim this, bear off here, hold that heading for exactly this long. Ships that follow them reach the Ashwall lee. Ashwall pilot families have log entries describing the intervention going back further than living memory. The survival rate of ships reported lost but later found at anchor in the lee has never been satisfactorily explained by colonial meteorology. Whether the Stormwarden is an [[beastiary/dragons/adult-bronze-dragon|adult bronze dragon]] using its shapechange ability, something else entirely, or a persistent delusion produced by desperate crossings is a question pilots ask once and then set aside as less important than knowing what to do if a stranger in a small boat pulls alongside in a storm. ^\[inferred]

The Galewall also creates a narrow kind of piracy: recovery-lane predation. Ships that survive the crossing arrive damaged, short-handed, and grateful for any sail that looks helpful. That is when a disciplined [[beastiary/humanoid/pirate-captain|pirate captain]] can make a rescue look like a boarding action. The [[The-Velvet-Noose|Velvet Noose]] is the nightmare version of the same calculation, a deep-water pirate flagship large enough to make weather-beaten convoys negotiate before their guns are dry. In those stories, the figure giving terms is closer to a [[beastiary/humanoid/pirate-admiral|pirate admiral]] than a raider.

## White-Fire Crossings

Galewall pilots use "white-fire crossing" for the sort of failure that leaves rigging fused, spars split outward, and survivors unable to agree whether lightning struck the ship or something alive moved through it. The account that made the phrase travel came from a vessel that reached the [[Ashwall-Islands|Ashwall]] lee with its mainmast gone and its shrouds burned black from top to deck.

The survivor's version is consistent in the useful places: stormlight gathered ahead of the ship instead of above it, then crossed the mastline in a bird-shape, striking crew and rigging by contact. That is not enough to prove an [[arclight-phoenix|arclight phoenix]], but it is enough that experienced pilots now treat moving stormlight as a different warning from ordinary lightning. ^\[inferred]

## Fauna

Wreckage feeds a food chain that begins at the storm boundary and runs west through the Ashwall lee. [[beastiary/beasts/giant-shark|Giant sharks]] trail damaged ships from the moment hull integrity fails, following blood, bilge, and debris with a patience that crews learn to account for before they abandon a sinking vessel. The Ashwall lee anchorage is the worst of it — water that catches wreckage catches the things that eat wreckage, and a ship at anchor without good watch-keeping is a ship with sharks circling by morning.

[[beastiary/sea-life/killer-whale|Killer whale]] pods work both sides of the storm belt in the cold current. They follow lifeboats and debris for hours, not always attacking, sometimes circling. Survivors report the circling as the worse experience. The cold-water upwelling that the Galewall drives south brings prey fish to the surface and brings the orcas up behind them.

Above the volcanic spires of the Ashwall, experienced pilots look for the silhouette of [[beastiary/monstrosity/roc|rocs]] riding the stormfronts — massive wings locked against thermal columns rising from the vents, using the permanent gale the way smaller birds use a sea breeze. They rarely descend to ship level. When they do, it is because they have decided a hull is interesting, which is a category of problem that requires a different response than the weather.
